# Getting the most out of it

`README.md` covers install. This covers the judgment: what to connect, the habits that make it compound instead of fizzle, and what to do when it's wrong.

---

## Two things about how this works

### The Project is the memory

Everything it learns about your business ends up as plain text files in one place, and that place *is* the memory. Two consequences:

- **Always start in the same Project.** Start somewhere else and it begins blind, with no idea who you are. This is the single most common mistake.
- **A scheduled brief needs a Project, not a folder.** The brief runs in the cloud each morning, and a folder on your laptop isn't reachable from there. Working in a folder is fine — you just ask for the brief rather than having it arrive.

### Every session starts fresh, except the files

It doesn't remember yesterday's conversation. It remembers yesterday's *files*.

That's the whole reason "wrap up" exists — it writes down what happened and what it learned, so tomorrow starts informed. Skip it for two weeks and you'll wonder why it feels forgetful.

## Setup, in one paragraph

Type `/setup`. It explains what it is, offers to take a name, then asks for your website and your own name and goes off to read about your business while you connect your calendar and email. It comes back with a draft of what it worked out, you correct it and answer two multiple-choice questions, and it builds the first picture of where things stand. Then it schedules two things — your weekday morning brief and your Sunday review — and sends a test to make sure delivery actually works.

About fifteen minutes. If you get interrupted, type `/setup` again — it picks up where it left off.

## What to connect, and in what order

Each grant is separate, and you can revoke any of them any time from your own account settings.

**1. Calendar — first.** The fastest grant, almost always works. Unlocks the shape of your day, who you've met recently, who you're about to meet, and prep items — the thing tomorrow that goes better if you read something today.

**2. Email — the important one.** Unlocks almost everything that makes this worth having: commitments you made and forgot, people waiting on you, threads that have gone quiet, and — critically — what you've *already sent*, which is how it avoids telling you to chase someone you emailed yesterday. **Without email, you have a calendar summariser.**

**3. Files — later, when you notice you need them.** Drive, OneDrive, Dropbox. Setup deliberately skips this so you're not clicking through three consent screens on your first run. Say "connect my files" when you want it. It unlocks proposals, contracts and budgets — and the single most valuable trick in the system: reading your own notes for promises you made in the first person, *"told him I'd look at it, haven't,"* which is where the oldest broken commitment in most businesses is hiding.

**4. One system of record — optional, high value.** Whatever the business actually runs on: a CRM, your books, a project tool. Connect only one, and make it the one you check most. Connecting your actual system beats connecting three generic ones.

## Overnight: it reads your history

Before your first brief arrives, it does one long pass on its own — six months of mail threads, a year of calendar. It's working out who you actually deal with, what people come to you for, what your weeks look like, what tends to precede the work that lands, and what falls through.

You don't do anything for this and you won't see it happen. It shows up as a first brief that already knows you, opening with two or three things it picked up. The rest goes into your context file under **How You Work**, **What Lands** and **What Falls Through**.

If you want it run again later — after a busy quarter, or when the business has shifted — say *"go through my history."*

---

## Adding a connector later is one sentence

Say *"connect my books"* — or your CRM, or wherever the work comes in — and that's the whole job. Every brief and every scheduled run from then on includes it. Nothing gets rebuilt, no task gets recreated, and you don't run setup again.

That's because the runs read the connected list out of your context file each time rather than a list frozen into the schedule. It also means the reverse is true: if a connection expires, say so and it stops being counted.

Worth doing when you notice the brief hedging about something — money it can only half-see, work it knows about but can't check.

## Correcting it is the whole mechanic

Open a chat in your Project and say what's wrong, in your own words. *"Marguerite isn't a client any more."* *"Stop calling them projects, they're engagements."* *"The brief keeps flagging the newsletter."* *"Send it at 6, not 7."*

It rewrites what it knows — not a note appended underneath the wrong thing, the actual entry changed — and then it tells you what it changed and what tomorrow will do differently. A correction reaches the scheduled brief and anything else running on its own, which is the part you can't see and shouldn't have to think about.

You are not filing a bug. This is the same conversation you'd have with a person who got something wrong, and it has the same effect.

## Day one: correct it

When the first picture appears, it asks: **"What did it get wrong?"**

Answer honestly and specifically. This isn't politeness — corrections get written into your context file permanently, so the same mistake can't come back. Five minutes here is worth more than anything else you'll do.

Worth correcting:

- Someone listed as a client who isn't one any more
- A name it thinks matters that doesn't
- The wrong word for something
- A priority order that's plainly wrong for how your business works
- Something it called urgent that genuinely isn't

Then say **"wrap up"** before you close it.

## Week one: the daily loop

Everything it sends is titled with your own first name — *Dana's Brief*, *Dana's Review*, *Dana's Primer* — and signed by whatever you called it. One search finds all of them.

**Morning — the brief arrives.** Read it. If something's wrong, say so next time you're in.

**During the day — talk to it normally.** "Draft a reply to Marcus." "What did I agree with the board in June?" No command language, no prompt formula.

**End of day — "wrap up."** Ten seconds. It logs what happened and updates what it knows.

**Sunday — the review arrives.** The whole picture rather than today's slice: what moved this week, what didn't, what's been circling, what's coming. It lands late Sunday afternoon on purpose — early enough that you can still do something about it before Monday. It ends with up to three things worth setting up, each declinable in a word.

You can also ask for it any time with **"where do things stand"**, which writes `Standing Map.md` and rebuilds the dashboard page on your desktop.

**Friday — the primer, if you took it.** One thing worth reading, picked from where your field, the work you actually do, and what's happening with AI overlap. One topic per week, never the same one twice, and everything it read is listed at the bottom with dates so you can check it yourself. Some weeks it's your industry; some weeks it's getting more out of this; some weeks it's what not to paste into a chat window.

The rule it runs on is that it researches the topic that week from sources it actually fetches — it never writes one from what it already knows. Which means some weeks nothing arrives, because nothing recent enough turned up to stand behind. That's deliberate, and the next brief tells you it happened. A confident, stale claim about a fast-moving field is worth less than silence.

Steer it in a sentence: *"cover agent security next"*, *"less about tooling"*, *"stop the primer."* It keeps a log of what it's covered in `Learning Log.md`, which is also where you can see what it's queued up.

**The dashboard page is yours to change.** It's built from `Dashboard Template.html` in your workspace. Open it, reorder the cards, drop one you don't use, or add your own by copying a card and giving it a line saying what belongs in it. Your version survives every rebuild. It'll also suggest sections now and then — an invoice tracker, a content list — when it notices work with nowhere to go.

**The page only rebuilds when you're at your machine**, because it lives on that machine and the scheduled runs happen in the cloud. The *picture* doesn't depend on it — that's `Standing Map.md`, which the Sunday run rewrites every week and which reads fine on a phone. Every build says when it happened, at the top.

**Hand it your dates.** "Remind me on the 12th." "Don't let me forget the board thing on the 9th." It arrives that morning as its own short email and then deletes itself, and it doesn't use up one of the three things it watches — so use it freely. It'll also spot dates you didn't hand over, like something you promised in a thread six weeks ago, and offer.

**The habit that decides whether this works:** correcting it when it's wrong. People who do that have a sharp system in three weeks. People who quietly ignore the wrong bits have an ignorable one.

## Week two: make it do things

**Teach it a job you repeat.** Say *"learn how I do my weekly client update"* — or the monthly funder report, the meeting prep, the invoice chase. It does the job with you once, you tell it what's wrong, and it writes a playbook with your corrections in it. After that: *"run my weekly client update."*

Good candidates are frequent, repetitive, and have a right answer you'd recognise on sight. Bad candidates are one-offs and anything where you can't say what good looks like.

**Give it something to watch.** Say *"keep an eye on the Hollis statements."* It checks every morning against your actual inbox and tells you the day they land. Three of these at most — a deliberate limit, so you can always say what's running on your behalf. Ask "what's running" any time, and "stop watching X" to retire one.

By default these *propose* rather than act. You can let one act on a specific named thing, and it'll tell you afterwards. Nothing that leaves — no sending, no replying, no calendar changes — is ever on the table.

## When it's wrong

| What's happening | What to do |
|---|---|
| The picture is thin or generic | Your context file is thin. Say "update your context" and tell it what's missing. |
| It keeps flagging things that aren't urgent | Tell it so, specifically. Priority adjusts from corrections. |
| It missed something obvious | Say "what can you see." Usually a missing connector. |
| It used the wrong word for something | Correct it once. It's permanent after that. |
| It suggested chasing someone you already emailed | Tell it. That's a real defect — every claim that someone hasn't replied is supposed to carry the sent-mail search that proves it. |
| No Sunday review arrived | The weekly task didn't run or wasn't created. Say "what's running" — it lists everything on a schedule. |
| No primer arrived | Either it found nothing recent enough to stand behind — the next brief says so in a line — or the task isn't running. Say "what's running" to tell which. |
| The primer keeps covering the same ground | Say so. It logs every topic in `Learning Log.md` to avoid exactly that, and "cover [X] next" puts something at the front of the queue. |
| It feels like it forgot everything | You probably started in a different Project, or haven't been saying "wrap up." |
| A stranger's sales email showed up as a priority | Tell it. It's supposed to hold those back, and that correction matters. |
| The brief is generic — wrong words, no history | The scheduled run couldn't reach your workspace. Open a session there and say "good morning"; it'll tell you what the schedule should point at. |
| The brief lands in Drafts, not your inbox | Your mail connection can write but not send. Setup should have told you this on day one. |
| "Good morning" answered as something else | Another plugin claims the phrase. Use `/aidgentic-chief-of-staff:morning-brief`. |
| `/setup` does nothing | The plugin toggle is off. Customize → Plugins → switch it on. |

## Habits that compound, and habits that kill it

**Compound:** correcting it out loud every time it's wrong · "wrap up" before you close · reading the brief before your inbox, not after · telling it things as they change rather than in batches · teaching it one repeated job a month.

**Kill it:** starting in a different Project each session · silently ignoring the parts that are wrong · treating the brief as something to skim · never connecting email, then concluding it isn't useful · expecting it to be good on day one, when it's built to get sharper and starts average.

---

## The one-line version

Connect your email. Correct it every time it's wrong. Say "wrap up" before you close.
